In May 2025 the Department of Defense formally accepted a Boeing 747-8
valued at roughly $400 million from the Qatari royal family, to serve as
an interim Air Force One. It arrived at Joint Base Andrews in June 2026
and was unveiled at a hangar ceremony as the "bridge" presidential
aircraft. The stated plan sends it to a presidential library after the
current term. It is among the most valuable gifts a foreign government
has ever given the United States.

This is the first ruling. Every input below cites a public record listed
at the end of this page. The score is an opinion, produced by applying
the published methodology to those records. The inputs are all listed
and open to dispute.

## The facts on record

**The gift.** In May 2025, the Secretary of Defense accepted a Boeing
747-8 from Qatar, described by the Pentagon as compliant with federal
rules and by the Air Force as an "unconditional donation." A new 747-8
runs approximately $400 million; this one, previously configured for the
Qatari royal family, had roughly 800 hours of flight time.

**The context.** Boeing's contract for two purpose-built replacement
Air Force One aircraft, signed in 2018 for $3.9 billion, is years
behind schedule, with delivery now projected around 2028. The existing
VC-25A fleet is roughly four decades old. The gifted jet fills that gap.

**The destination.** Administration statements indicate the aircraft
would be donated to a future presidential library after the president
leaves office, rather than remaining in the Air Force fleet.

**The timing.** The same week the gift was announced, during a
presidential visit to Doha, Qatar Airways announced an order for up to
210 Boeing aircraft. The Index does not assert the two events are
connected. It scores their documented proximity, nothing further.

**The dispute.** The Constitution's foreign emoluments clause requires
the consent of Congress before a federal officeholder accepts a present
from a foreign state. Congress held no vote. The administration's
position is that the gift was made to the Defense Department, not the
president personally, and complied with applicable law. Members of
Congress and ethics experts publicly disputed both the ethics and the
legality of the acceptance. That dispute remains unresolved.

**The transparency.** None of this was hidden. The gift was announced on
the president's own social media account, defended repeatedly on the
record, and celebrated at a public unveiling.

## The ruling, factor by factor

**F1 · Benefit Magnitude (9/10, ×3.0).** At ~$400 million, this sits at
the extreme end of any gift ever accepted by a US administration. It is
not a 10 only because the methodology reserves the ceiling for benefits
that are effectively unbounded (ongoing revenue streams, equity stakes).
A one-time asset, however enormous, caps at 9.

**F2 · Personal Accrual (6/10, ×2.5).** On paper the aircraft is a
Defense Department asset, which holds the score down. The stated
endpoint pushes it back up: the plan sends the jet to a presidential
library once the term ends, and a library is a private nonprofit tied
to a single individual, so the asset stays in the official's orbit
after they leave office. A straight government-to-government gift with
no personal tail would score 2 or 3. A jet deeded directly to the
individual would score 9 or 10. This case sits between the two.

**F3 · Source Conflict (9/10, ×2.0).** The donor is a foreign state
with active, ongoing interests before the US government: defense
cooperation, basing, and trade. A nine-figure aircraft order involving a
major US manufacturer was announced within days of the gift. The
methodology does not require proving intent. It scores the documented
density of the donor's interests, and that density is near the maximum.

**F4 · Process Integrity (7/10, ×1.5).** The constitutional mechanism
for foreign gifts to officeholders, congressional consent, was not
used. The administration asserts an alternative legal theory
(department-level acceptance); that theory is contested by ethics
experts and legislators and has not been tested in court. Untested is
not the same as violated, which is why this scores 7 rather than 9–10.
A congressional vote approving the gift would have scored this 1–2,
regardless of the outcome.

**F5 · Concealment (2/10, ×1.0).** The gift was announced, defended,
and unveiled, and reporters were invited aboard. The score is not zero
because the full terms of the transfer agreement were not immediately
made public, but by the standard of concealment this is close to the
floor.

## The receipt

(9×3.0) + (6×2.5) + (9×2.0) + (7×1.5) + (2×1.0) = **73 Nixons**

## Why not higher?

Factor F5. Nothing here was hidden. Watergate maxed F5 because the
cover-up was the crime. A 73 with everything in the open is a different
kind of finding than a 73 built on concealment, and the factor
breakdown preserves that difference. The Index measures conduct, not
volume.

## What would change the score

The methodology commits us to revising rulings as the record develops.
This score moves if: the aircraft remains permanently in the Air Force
fleet instead of transferring to a library (F2 drops, total falls to
roughly 62); Congress votes to consent retroactively (F4 drops); a
court rules on the legality either way (F4 moves in the corresponding
direction); or documents emerge showing undisclosed terms (F5 rises).
Revisions get their own entries. The math stays public either way.
